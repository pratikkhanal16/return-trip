Could the boat that brings food also take the toilet waste back?

When a family's toilet floods, they use a toilet chair with a bucket. A trained worker brings a clean bucket and takes the closed, used one away by boat. The waste then goes to a treatment site.

Our idea is to use relief boat trips that already run. Whether those boats have room, and whether their routes fit, still has to be checked with the people who run them.

In one minute
  1. Before the flood: each home in a flood-prone ward gets a toilet kit, which is a chair, sealed buckets and a yellow flag.
  2. During the flood: the family uses the chair upstairs. When the bucket is full, they close it and raise the flag.
  3. The boat that brings food takes the closed bucket and leaves a clean one. Food and waste stay in separate ends of the boat.
  4. On dry land, the bucket goes to a treatment site. Staff there sign for it on arrival, so we know where it went.
The problem

Flood relief reaches the house. Sanitation doesn't.

When water rises past the ground floor, the toilet is the first thing lost and the last thing fixed. Families move upstairs and use the floodwater, a plastic bag, or nothing. Older people, wheelchair users and women are hit hardest. Many drink less and wait for dark.

Relief agencies are good at getting supplies in. Nobody plans for the waste that has to come back out. That waste enters the same water people wade through, and the risk of diarrhoea and cholera rises in the weeks after a flood.

The September 2024 floods in Nepal killed more than 200 people and severely affected thousands of families in informal settlements around Kathmandu and Lalitpur.8 The same pattern repeats in Thailand, Bangladesh, Pakistan and Mozambique.

How it works

Mina's bucket: a flood story in six steps.

Mina is 9. She lives in House 5 with her parents and her grandmother, Yai, who walks with a stick. Follow their toilet bucket from home to the treatment site. The drawing changes as you scroll.

Sheet 1 of 6 · Section A–ART-412 · not to scale
Step 1 · Day 1

The water comes in. The toilet disappears.

Overnight the river rises. In the morning, brown water fills the whole ground floor, and the family toilet is underwater. Mina, her parents and Yai carry their things upstairs.

Upstairs is dry, but there is no toilet. "Where do we go now?" Mina asks.

The water is 1.1 m deep, and the toilet is 0.8 m below the surface. Homes with only one floor would get a raised platform for the chair, strapped to a wall. It still needs a stability test.

Step 2 · Day 1, afternoon

The boat brings rice, water, and a toilet.

The relief boat stops at the window. Along with the rice, the crew hands up a toilet chair with armrests, so Yai can sit and stand on her own. It comes with a bucket with a tight lid, a bag of sawdust to cover each use, and a yellow flag.

The bucket has its own number: RT-412. "That's our bucket's name," Mina says.

A line on the bucket marks 15 L, so a full bucket weighs about 15 kg. That is a design assumption and needs a lifting check.9 Refills also bring sanitary pads and diapers.

Step 3 · Day 3, morning

The bucket is full. Up goes the yellow flag!

Two days later, the bucket reaches the line. Mina's mum closes the lid. Mina ties the yellow flag to the balcony, where the boat can see it from far away.

Her mum also taps Pickup in LINE. Homes without a phone just use the flag. Either way, House 5 goes on today's boat list.

Step 4 · Day 3, 10:00

Food in the front. Buckets in the back.

The same relief boat sets out again. Food and drinking water ride at the front. At the back, behind a screen, is a strong rack that holds six closed buckets.

There are two helpers with two jobs. Ploy only touches food and clean buckets. Ton wears gloves and boots, and only touches full buckets. The two never mix.

Six full buckets weigh about 90 kg. The load and the boat's balance need checking for each boat type. The crew's list shows only house numbers, never names.

Step 5 · Day 3, 10:40, then 15:20

Uh-oh, the lid isn't shut! Try again.

Ton checks the bucket and finds the lid hasn't clicked shut. The rule is simple: a bucket that could leak never goes on a food boat. He gives the family a new lid, shows Mina how to press it until it clicks, and promises to come back.

At 15:20 the boat returns. Click, sealed. Not too full. No leaks. Into the rack it goes, and Mina gets a clean bucket.

Step 6 · Day 3, evening

Bucket RT-412 arrives safely, and someone signs for it.

On dry land, a covered truck carries the buckets to a waste treatment site. A worker there checks the number, RT-412, and signs a receipt: RCV-1117-412.

Mina's mum gets a message: "Your bucket arrived safely." No waste went into the water the family wades through.

The receipt shows the bucket arrived. "Treatment confirmed" is a separate record, which the site adds once the waste is treated. No site has agreed yet. Finding one is our first task.

On the phone

Three screens, one record for each bucket.

The family asks for a pickup in LINE, or raises the flag. The boat crew works from a short list. The subdistrict office sees every bucket from request to treatment. Tap the buttons to try it, or press Play. All names and numbers are examples.

What's new

One idea: collect toilet buckets on boat trips that already happen.

Bucket toilets and bucket collection already work in cities like Cap-Haïtien, Haiti.1 Flood relief boats already visit cut-off homes every day. Nobody has joined the two. Return Trip adds one trained handler and a sealed rack to an existing relief run, so the boat can take full buckets back to dry land.

What it addsOne trained handler, a six-slot rack, buckets with lids, and a signed record for each bucket.
What it doesn't needA new fleet, sewers or roads. The family doesn't need a smartphone.
What it costs (estimate)About ฿135 per home per flood day for 12 homes, falling to about ฿67 for 40 homes. The subdistrict pays, and families pay nothing.
What must be checkedThat relief boats have room and suitable routes, that a receiving site agrees, and that lifting and boat balance are safe.

Crews stop collecting when:

  • the current is too fast for the boat to hold position at a house
  • a bucket leaks, or its seal is broken
  • the transfer point or treatment site is closed
  • the handler has no gloves, spill kit or clean buckets

First test: a dry run

Run a full day with water and sawdust instead of waste: 12 homes, 1 boat run (as in the cost model), and a full lift, carry, swap and truck cycle. We redesign if a bucket spills, if a lift goes over 15 kg, if a swap takes longer than 4 minutes, or if any user can't sit and stand without help.

Ideas for later (not part of the first pilot)

Each of these could be added once the basic service works. We keep them out of the pilot so it tests one thing.

Later 1Research option

Urea in the bucket

Lab studies show that urea added to stored faeces releases ammonia, which kills germs.2,3 It has never been tested in this bucket or this service, so we make no safety claim for it.

Later 2Research option

Welfare checks on quiet homes

A home that should be asking for swaps but stays quiet might need a visit. People are known to eat and drink less when there is no safe toilet.4 This needs a partner with a welfare mandate.

Later 3Research option

Kits sent out on the flood forecast

Move kits to ward depots when the government flood alert is issued. This follows evidence from anticipatory cash transfers in Bangladesh.5

Later 4Research option

Disease sampling at the jetty

Pooled samples from each ward could be tested for cholera. This would follow WHO guidance6 and recent work in camps without sewers.7 It would need a lab partner and an ethics review.
